## Why should we care?

Acceleration structures are a crucial development in ray tracing renderers. Without them, rendering would be too slow to be viable because of the large number of ray-primitive intersection tests required. With acceleration structures, it is possible to significantly increase rendering speed by reducing the number of intersection tests, which are the most expensive part, and replacing them with faster tests against the acceleration structure. Figure 1 shows the three structures we built, partitioning the same handful of triangles.

In this project, we augment an existing path tracing renderer with these acceleration structures and compare their efficiency:

1. **Can we build all three?** A uniform grid, a Bounding Volume Hierarchy (BVH) with median splits, and a BVH with the surface area heuristic (SAH).
2. **Which one is most efficient, and where?** Measured as the average traversal steps and intersection tests per ray, on a dense mesh and on three scenes picked to stress each structure.

We expected the grid to be the slowest of the three, and the BVH with SAH to be the fastest.

*Note: the renderer is [valentecaio's path tracer](https://github.com/valentecaio/raytracer), based on Peter Shirley et al.'s Ray Tracing in One Weekend series with extra features: PBR and Phong materials, Russian roulette, stratified sampling, depth of field, OpenMP parallelization and a basic `.obj` loader. Our contributions are solely the acceleration structures, plus minor tweaks elsewhere for compatibility and ease of experimentation.*

## The grid

### Construction

Construction begins by creating a scene bounding box that contains all primitives in the scene; this is the space we construct our grid on. We use the heuristic `∛(number of primitives)` to determine the number of cells per axis, which yields an average of one primitive per cell. The dimensions of a cell then follow from the scene bounding box and the number of cells per axis. The final step iterates through each primitive, determines which cells its bounding box lies within, and stores a reference to the primitive in those cells. Each cell is a small struct holding the indices of its primitives.

### Traversal

Grid traversal is the 3D-DDA algorithm of Amanatides and Woo (Figure 2). It first finds the ray's entry cell into the scene bounding box; if the ray misses the box, we simply move on to the next ray. If the entry cell contains primitives, an intersection test is performed on all of them. The algorithm then determines which axis boundary will be crossed next by comparing `tMaxX`, `tMaxY` and `tMaxZ`. The cell coordinate is stepped along the axis with the smallest `tMax`, and that axis's `tMax` is advanced by `tDelta` to point to the next boundary. If an intersection has been found and the distance to the next cell is larger than the closest hit so far, we terminate early. Otherwise, the algorithm ends when the ray exits the scene bounding box.

## The BVH

The BVH in this project is a hybrid of Bikker's tutorial and Shirley's *Ray Tracing: The Next Week*.

### Construction

Construction begins by creating a pool of `2N − 1` BVH nodes, where `N` is the number of primitives in the scene. A head node stores an axis-aligned bounding box (AABB) containing all primitives, an integer pointing to its left child, and the number of primitives in the node. Construction then recursively subdivides at the midpoint of the longest axis of the current node: primitives whose position along that axis is less than the midpoint go to the left, the rest to the right. Bounding boxes are created for both sides, and these become the left and right children. This continues until a node holds a single primitive, which becomes a leaf.

### Traversal

Traversal begins at the head node. If the ray intersects its AABB, we check intersection with its children, and recursively repeat until the ray either misses every remaining AABB or reaches a leaf, where we perform an intersection test with the primitive.

### Splitting with the SAH

The BVH-SAH differs only in where it splits an AABB, instead of always using the midpoint. We test discrete, evenly spaced split points: for each axis and each candidate, we compute the SAH cost `N_left · A_left + N_right · A_right`, where `N_left` is the number of primitives (by centroid) left of the split and `A_left` is the area of their AABB. The split point and axis with the lowest cost win. Traversal is the same as for the regular BVH.

The results show a large trade-off between traversal cost and intersection cost. The grid consistently has fewer traversal steps, averaging between 1.11 and 6.82, whereas the BVH averages between 17.41 and 33.55. For intersection tests it is the other way around: the BVH averages between 0.33 and 2.01, whilst the grid averages between 15,479 and 16,973.

These results are to be expected. The grid has fewer traversal steps because we traverse it linearly, so a ray crosses at most slightly more cells than there are along one axis, and early termination cuts this further. The BVH, on the other hand, must traverse a deep hierarchy to find out whether it hits a primitive at all. For intersection tests, a single grid cell may contain many primitives, and a primitive may overlap several cells, so entering a cell already means multiple tests, sometimes repeated for the same primitive. The BVH only tests a primitive when it reaches a leaf deep in the hierarchy.

### BVH vs. BVH-SAH

On the same scene and views, BVH and BVH-SAH only differ in their traversal statistics: BVH-SAH takes consistently fewer traversal steps (Figure 4). This is to be expected, since SAH picks the split that minimizes a cost based on the surface areas of the resulting boxes and the number of primitives in each child. This gives a more balanced tree with tighter boxes that follow the geometry better, so irrelevant subtrees are abandoned earlier. Intersection statistics stay the same, since the rays still reach the same leaf nodes, just organized differently in the hierarchy.

### Three scenes, three challenges

To highlight the different challenges each structure faces, we ran three more scenes, each from four viewpoints (Figure 5):

- **A dense object in a void.** The Stanford Bunny (~69,000 triangles) on its own: a control scene for a single mesh surrounded by empty space. The BVH is extremely efficient here, wrapping the bunny in a box and skipping most of the empty space at the root. The grid must step through empty cells too, taking around 2 to 4 times more traversal steps. The biggest difference is in intersection tests: the bunny is dense and small relative to the world, so many triangles fall into the same cells and get tested every time a ray passes through, with peaks of 11,291 tests for a single ray. The BVH's peaks stay at 11 to 19.
- **Sparse geometry.** Two bunnies far apart, separated by a wide empty gap. This shows the fundamental difference between space partitioning and object partitioning: the grid fills the gap with cells that a ray must step through, while the BVH's root likely splits into one child per bunny, so a ray through the middle misses both and stops immediately, with around 3 to 4 times fewer traversal steps. The grid again packs each bunny's triangles into large cells, tested on average 5 to 26 times per ray, with peaks up to 18,228; the BVH averages close to 0.
- **Uniformly distributed objects.** 7,000 randomly positioned and rotated quads (14,000 triangles): dense, overlapping geometry with little empty space. In theory this helps the grid and challenges the BVH, whose boxes now overlap. As expected, the BVH's average traversal spiked to around 74 steps, since no split cleanly sends a ray down only one child, while the grid averaged 17.63 to 23.91 steps. Still, even in the grid's best case, the BVH wins on raw efficiency, averaging 0.30 to 0.53 intersection tests against the grid's 38.85 to 47.30.

## Next steps

- **More scene types**, and a look at how the structures hold up with instancing.
- **The GPU.** On a GPU, the trade-off between traversal and intersection probably looks completely different again. I took the BVH there next, in [From BVH to Linear BVH](/projects/lbvh).

## Takeaways

If I had to sum up the whole exercise in one sentence: space partitioning (the grid) is cheap to traverse but blind to how geometry clusters, while object partitioning (the BVH) pays more per traversal step in exchange for near-perfect intersection efficiency, and in every scene we tested, that trade was worth making, even in the grid's best case. SAH is basically "BVH, but it also thought about where to cut."
