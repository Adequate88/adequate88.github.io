## Why should we care?

Being able to create distinct moods and emotions is crucial in fields like animation, game design and film production, which makes it both technically and artistically compelling. In this project, we set out to create visually captivating scenes that evoke distinct moods through rendering techniques alone: particle effects for fire, smoke and fireflies, bloom for enhanced lighting, fog to set a mood, and toon shading to stylize (Figure 1). The idea is to combine these shading and rendering techniques to turn an otherwise simple scene into something artistically appealing:

1. **Can rendering alone set a mood?** One simple scene, made to feel completely different by the effects layered on top.
2. **Can you change it live?** Alongside preset moods, every effect's parameters are exposed in the app's UI, so users can create their own moods and atmospheres.

The main challenge turned out to be balancing all those parameters into aesthetically pleasing compositions.

## The scene

The scene needed to be simple but versatile: a base for all the effects, and a setting that suits a variety of moods. Catalina recreated a forest in Blender, following a tutorial: the ground, a river, three types of trees, rocks, and a space for the campfire. Since the framework could only give one material to each `.obj` file, every object of a single material became its own file, and its Blender colour became a material in our code.

## Particle effects

Our particle systems are built from a few classes. `particle_system.js` holds the main class, which stores all particles in a `particle_container` array. When it is created, parameters such as lifetime, colour, scale, velocity, the maximum particle count and the number of new particles per frame can be customized. Its `evolve(dt)` function iterates through each particle and updates its position, velocity, colour or other attributes, and `respawn_particle()` replaces particles that have outlived their lifetime, based on user-defined preferences such as random colours or velocities. Instead of deleting particles, it resets the values of the oldest particles past their lifetime (Figure 2). Each particle also carries its own colour and scale, so the system's values act as an average and every particle gets its own offset, which gives the particles a more dynamic feel.

Fire, smoke and fireflies are child classes of `ParticleSystem` that override `evolve` and `respawn_particle` to get their own physics and behaviour. In a scene, the chosen particle system is created and its parameters adjusted, and all its particles are added to the scene's `billboard_objects` array. This lets billboards, and so particles, be handled and rendered separately from all other objects.

A dedicated renderer, `billboard_sr.js`, then iterates through `billboard_objects`. For each billboard it computes four vertices from the camera's up and right vectors, defining a quad around the object's centre that always faces the camera (Figure 3). The vertices, colours, textures and view-projection matrix are passed to the billboard shaders: the vertex shader multiplies the vertex positions by the view-projection matrix and passes on the texture coordinates, and the fragment shader uses the particle's base colour, or maps its texture onto the quad.

## Normal mapping

Normal mapping touches four files (Figure 4):

1. **`cg_mesh.js`.** A new `compute_mapping_vectors(mesh, faces)` computes a tangent and bitangent for each vertex. It iterates over every triangle and uses its vertices to compute a tangent and bitangent for each of them, stored in a list per vertex. Since vertices can be part of several faces, most end up with several; we simply take their average. All functions in `cg_mesh` now compute these vectors and pass them on as properties of the mesh.
2. **`blinn_phong_sr.js`.** The renderer passes the vertex tangent and bitangent to the shader as attributes, plus an `is_normaled` uniform to turn normal mapping on and off, and the normal map itself if the object has one.
3. **`blinn_phong.vert.glsl`.** The `mat_normals_model_view` transform is applied to the normal, tangent and bitangent, which are normalized and passed to the fragment shader.
4. **`blinn_phong.frag.glsl`.** The fragment shader builds the TBN matrix from these three vectors and samples the normal map like any 2D texture, using the object's UVs. The sample is scaled by 2 and shifted by −1 to map it from [0, 1] to [−1, 1], the range of a normal's components, normalized, and transformed by the TBN matrix. The result is the normal for the rest of the Blinn-Phong lighting.

## Bloom, fog and toon shading

The other three effects were built by my teammates, and they are what give each mood most of its character.

**Bloom** (Tizian) follows four steps: render the image without bloom, extract the bright regions into another texture, blur that texture, and add it back onto the original (Figure 5). Brightness is weighted towards red, to make the campfire glow. The blur is a two-pass Gaussian blur, horizontal then vertical, repeated a few times and sped up with linear sampling; the UI controls the number of passes and the brightness threshold, and can show each intermediate step.

**Fog** (Tizian) computes each fragment's distance to the camera in view space and mixes the fog colour in by a fog factor: linear between a start and end distance, or one of two exponential falloffs controlled by a density. The colour, the factor type and its parameters are all in the UI, along with an option to keep the sky sphere out of the fog.

**Toon shading** (Catalina) only changes the Blinn-Phong fragment shader. The diffuse factor is snapped to a limited number of levels, the ceiling of the diffuse value times the number of levels, divided by that number again, and the specular term becomes a step: zero, unless the original value is 0.98 or higher, where it becomes 1. Keeping those few highlights adds to the cartoony style.

## Next steps

- **Fog over particles.** Fog doesn't affect the particles (Figure 6): they have no mesh, so the fog's vertex shader has nothing to work on. Giving each particle a mesh would complicate the particle system and cost a lot of performance, given how many particles a scene can have, so we left it after a few attempts. It's the first thing to revisit.
- **Blur artifacts.** At a low number of blur passes, grid-like artifacts appear in the blurred bright regions. More passes remove them, at a noticeable cost when bloom is combined with other effects; other sampling methods and UV calculations didn't help.
